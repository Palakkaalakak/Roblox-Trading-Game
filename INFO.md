# Game Info — TBD Trading (Roblox)

An exhaustive overview of a Roblox item-trading economy game. "TBD Trading" is a placeholder name. This is the full brief — every system, every number, and the reasoning behind each design decision.

## What the game is

There is no combat, no obstacle course, no simulator-clicker loop. The entire game is: obtain items (gacha "rolls," crafting, or a fixed-price store), hold a collection whose value moves with a live supply/demand economy, and trade — directly with another player, via a public trade board, or via player-run shop booths.

**The core hook, the actual reason to play at all, is Community Rewards** (detailed fully under Monetization below): gems accumulated in the game are meant to eventually be redeemable, for players who reach a threshold and pass review, for real Robux paid out through the developers' group. Everything else in the game — the economy, the trading, the crafting ladder, the syndicates — is the vehicle for that. Without a real payoff at the top, there's no strong answer to "why actually play this over anything else"; Community Rewards is meant to be that answer. It's also the reason the in-game economy is so strictly locked down (gems can only ever be minted by a real purchase or a rewarded-ad watch, never by any gameplay action) — that invariant is what makes the eventual payout mathematically safe for the developers to offer at all, since a deliberately lopsided buy/redeem exchange rate guarantees the total ever paid out can never exceed the total actually spent by players.

## The plaza — what the world actually looks like

The game world is one open plaza (a single low-poly Roblox baseplate area, no zones/floors to unlock), populated with **physical, walk-up NPCs and structures** rather than menu buttons for everything:

- **Shopkeeper NPC** — a standing character model next to a counter and a hanging sign ("Limited Store"). Walking up and holding E opens the Store.
- **Roll Master NPC** — same pattern, opens the gacha Rolls panel.
- **Event Host NPC** — opens the seasonal/limited Events panel.
- **Trade Board Pillar** — a tall pillar with a pinned-notice-style sign reading "TRADE BOARD," holding E opens the public board.
- **Three physical leaderboard boards** standing in the world (Portfolio Value, Total Trades, Rarest Item), each a screen-like model rendering the live top players client-side, with the viewer's own row highlighted even off the visible page. They're geometry only — no scripts of their own — and bind to which board they show via a `BoardId` attribute rather than by model name, so a builder can move, rename, or duplicate one in Studio without touching code. Rendered client-side deliberately: the rows differ per viewer (your own row is highlighted), and replicating a SurfaceGui full of frames from the server for something every client can draw itself from one remote call would just be wasted bandwidth.
- **Player-claimed trade booths** scattered around — hand-placed stall models a player claims (hold E), each with a SurfaceGui storefront face showing their stocked items with prices. Booths are never spawned by code; adding or moving one is purely a Studio edit — the service just discovers tagged models at boot.
- **AFK zone pads** — green floor pads, several placed around the map, capped at 6 players each (overflow routes to the next zone by fill order). Standing on one freezes your character and camera in place (GUI stays live) while a "Go AFK" panel runs.
- **A wooden AFK sign** planted in the middle of the plaza — walking up and pressing E opens the Go AFK panel directly, instead of a HUD button. AFK access was deliberately moved from a sidebar icon to a physical world object, matching how Store/Rolls/Events/Board are all NPCs/props rather than menu buttons.

The sidebar HUD (screen-anchored, right edge) is reserved for things that aren't tied to a place in the world: Inventory, Trade (opens the picker), Profiles, Shop (Robux), Free Gems, Group (syndicate) — six icon buttons, evenly spaced. That split (world objects for the economy loop, menu buttons for account-level screens) is a deliberate pattern, not an accident — the world stays worth walking around in.

## The gacha roll experience

Opening the Rolls panel shows five tier cards with live per-item odds. Pulling the trigger plays a **case-opening / slot-machine reel animation** — a horizontal strip of item icons scrolls past a fixed center marker and decelerates onto the prize over about 4.5 seconds, heavily eased so the last second feels like it's "almost" stopping on each tile it creeps past before finally landing (the same reveal language used by Roblox gacha games like Bloxfruits' fruit-dealer wheel and most "case opening" simulators). Two specific psychology beats are built into the reel on purpose, researched from gacha/slot-machine design:

- **Near-miss** — the strip is seeded so a high-rarity icon sits directly next to the landing slot, so you visibly see what you almost got.
- **Back-loaded deceleration** — the scroll spends most of its time crawling rather than flying by, stretching out the anticipation right before the reveal.

Landing flashes a bright payoff pop timed to the exact instant the reel stops, then shows the item name in its rarity color plus its value in gems, with an "again" button right there to chain another pull.

## Core loop

1. Get items — roll gacha (gems), craft them from other items, buy them from the fixed-price Store, or earn them via AFK zones.
2. Hold a collection whose Estimated Value tracks a live economy, not fixed dev-set prices.
3. Trade — 1:1 direct trades, the public trade board (swap / bulk-buy / sell-shop listings), or booths.
4. Spend gems to roll/craft more, or spend Robux to boost trade-ad visibility or buy cosmetics.
5. Syndicates, leaderboards, and milestones sit on top and reward doing steps 1–4.
6. Build up gems toward the Community Rewards threshold — the actual long-term payoff loop everything above feeds into.

Framing matters here, worth researching directly: buying gems isn't pitched to players as a purely selfish transaction ("pay us, get gems"), it's closer to a donation-drive appeal — spending supports the game/community, and the eventual Community Rewards payout is the shared thing that spending makes possible, not just a personal purchase. That's a different psychological pitch than a standard gem-shop upsell, closer to how creator/charity donation drives frame giving as supporting something bigger than the individual transaction, and it's worth researching how other games or platforms successfully pull that off without it reading as manipulative.

## The economy engine

Every item has a live Estimated Value, a fixed lifetime supply cap (`totalSupply`, set once at creation and never changed), and a "reserve" of undistributed copies. Items enter circulation by being drawn from reserve — rolls, crafting outputs, and store buys all pull from the same reserve via one atomic cross-server operation, so the same "last copy" can never be handed to two players on different servers at once. Items leave circulation ("burn") through crafting inputs (consumed and redistributed, not destroyed — see Crafting below) and dedicated sacrifice recipes (true sinks, nothing comes back).

**Value formula, as actually implemented:** `Value = max(1, floor(base * demandFactor + 0.5))`, where `demandFactor = 1 + DemandCoefficient * min(demand / DemandRef, 1)` (currently `DemandCoefficient = 0.5`, `DemandRef = 5`, so a red-hot item can be worth up to 1.5x its base). `base` is the item's rarity anchor price, or — for crafted/event items — `(sum of input values + input gems) * CraftValuePremium` (currently 1.10, a genuine +10% value-add for crafting). Demand itself is an exponentially-decaying moving average with a 6-hour half-life, bumped by +1.0 per closed trade containing the item and +0.2 whenever it's placed into a live trade-ad offer — so an item doesn't need to actually sell to register demand, just get listed.

*Worth flagging honestly:* the codebase's own comments describe an additional scarcity multiplier — `((circulating+burned)/circulating)^SupplyExponent`, capped at 6x — as part of the intended formula, and `EconomyConfig` documents it at length as core design ("a sink lifts EVERY holder... non-zero-sum"). But the actual `computeValue` function that runs at runtime only takes `(base, demand)` — no scarcity term currently executes. This looks like a genuine doc/implementation drift, not a deliberate simplification, and is worth resolving one way or the other.

**RAP** (recent average price) is tracked separately as an *observed* statistic from real gem-denominated trade settlements — imputed pro-rata across items when a trade includes non-gem items on both sides (each item's share of the imputed price is proportional to its own live Value within the offer), and skipped entirely (demand-bump only, no price sample) for a pure one-sided "gift" trade, since a gift carries no real price signal. RAP deliberately never feeds back into Value; an earlier version blended a value seed with RAP with decaying weight so Value asymptotically *became* RAP — a feedback loop with no anchor — and that coupling was removed on purpose.

**Storage split:** reserve/circulating counts (correctness-critical) go through the strong atomic path — a DataStore compare-and-swap write per reservation. RAP and demand (high-frequency, statistical) live in a local cache with a dirty flag instead, flushed durably every 60 seconds, last-writer-wins by timestamp — readers are at most one flush stale, which is an acceptable tradeoff for numbers nobody needs split-second-fresh. A separate in-memory-only "lazy" path exists for specific burst scenarios (large admin batch operations), with its own narrower, explicitly documented correctness tradeoff each time it's used. No economy state lives in MemoryStore at all; that quota is experience-wide and tiny (about 64KB in Studio) and is reserved entirely for the trade board, which needs it far more.

## Getting items, and why each path exists

There are four ways to get an item, each a deliberately different lever on the economy.

### Rolls (gacha)

The main *paid* path new goods enter the economy through — nothing appears on a timer, every item can be traced back to gems someone actually earned. 5 tiers, gems only, no pity system, straight weighted odds:

| Tier | Cost | Value floor | Rarity window | Odds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basic | 12 gems | 35% of cost | Common/Rare/Legendary/Mythical | 96.0 / 3.2 / 0.6 / 0.2% |
| Solid | 25 gems | 42% of cost | same | 74.0 / 21.0 / 4.0 / 1.0% |
| Lucky | 55 gems | 46% of cost | Rare/Legendary/Mythical/Godly | 73.0 / 21.0 / 5.0 / 1.0% |
| Grand | 110 gems | 50% of cost | same | 40.0 / 38.0 / 17.0 / 5.0% |
| Mythic | 240 gems | 52% of cost | Legendary/Mythical/Godly/Celestial | 50.0 / 33.0 / 12.0 / 5.0% |

Windows deliberately slide up the ladder as tiers get pricier — the cheap tiers literally cannot roll the top rarities, and the expensive tiers can't roll commons at all. The "value floor" is a genuine guarantee, not marketing — a roll will never return an item worth less than that fraction of what you paid; items below the floor are excluded from that tier's pool for that rarity entirely, and the floor is a property of the *tier*, not of whichever rarity happened to drop, so a Mythic roll guarantees most of its cost back no matter which of its four rarities lands.

Rolls draw from a "pool" — a rotating set of 5 items (a fixed 14-item set for the very first pool, procedurally generated ones afterward) spanning all six rarities, which is the *entire* rollable set at any moment. The advertised odds are exact and can't drift: how many copies of an item remain never changes its drop chance (the split within a rarity uses a fixed weight derived from the item's supply cap at creation, never its current remaining count), and the pool is replaced the instant *any one* of its items is fully exhausted, rather than waiting for the whole pool to run dry. The reasoning: while every item in a rarity still has at least one copy, the odds are exactly what's advertised no matter how lopsided the counts get; the moment one item hits zero, it would otherwise drop out of its rarity's weight split and every other item in that rarity would become likelier than advertised without anyone agreeing to that — both a lie to the player and a farmable exploit (drain the item you don't want, the rest improve). If a roll's random draw happens to land on a rarity that's momentarily empty, the roll is refused and refunded rather than quietly sliding into a neighboring rarity — the player is charged nothing and a fresh pool is minted in the background.

A retired pool isn't destroyed. Crafting inputs and roll item-payments redistribute consumed copies back to the item (and therefore the pool) they came from, so a retired pool slowly refills on its own. Once it's recovered at least 20% of the total supply it was minted with, *and* every item in it holds at least one copy again, it's revived and preferred over minting an entirely new pool — nothing is ever permanently thrown away, and old lineups can genuinely come back into rotation.

A roll can also be paid in items instead of gems, as long as their combined live Value covers the tier price (up to 12 distinct items, 99 copies each); those items are redistributed back into reserve the same way crafting inputs are. Items from *ended* limited/seasonal events are refused as payment specifically because pushing one back into reserve after its season closed would quietly undo the "this can never be made again" promise.

### Crafting

The *only* mechanic that actually creates value rather than moving it around — trading is zero-sum (items just change hands), a craft consumes inputs and mints an output seeded above their combined worth (currently +10%), so every completed craft genuinely grows the economy. Crafting costs items only, never a gem fee, since gems only ever enter the game through ads and Robux and should never double as a raw material. This is why the system is structured as a ladder — commons → refined materials → mid-tier Artifacts → capstone Grails, each rung worth more than the last:

- **Alchemist's Lab** (permanent, always-on, deliberately cheap) — the on-ramp: Transmutation Stone and Rune of Protection, exempt from the "should be hard to farm" logic on purpose. Every new player needs at least one recipe they can always make, win or lose on rotation — that's the on-ramp, not a whale target.
- **The Refinery** (permanent, tier 1) — 4 recipes, each turning 15 copies of one common into a refined material capped at 2,500 total supply: Cut Stone, Copper Ingot, Amber Resin, Fired Clay. Deliberately scarce even as a "basic" material — flood it and it's worthless.
- **Artisan Bench** (permanent, tier 1–2) — 8 mid-tier recipes producing Storm/Ember/Frost/Gilded Artifacts (900/900/900/500 supply) and Sun/Void Fragments and Sunforged/Voidforged Cores (700/700/300/300 supply) — every one of the ladder's middle rungs.
- **Rotating recipes** — 4 of a 6-recipe pool (Storm Totem, Ember Idol, Frost Crown, Gilded Mask, Essence Lantern, Relic Cairn) active at any time, on a strict 10-minute rotation, computed purely from the clock — every server agrees automatically, nothing to break on rotation, no stored state at all. By rule, a rotating recipe may only ever produce a terminal item nothing else depends on, so a window closing can never strand another recipe chain that needs its output as an ingredient. The rotation also guarantees at least one active tier-1 material producer at all times, so a fresh economy can never stall on a window that happens to only pick higher-tier recipes.
- **Shadow Ritual** (sacrifice recipes) — the heaviest true item sinks in the game: 30 Copper Coin → Shadow Crown (220 supply), 30 Stone Relic → Hollow Effigy (180 supply). These genuinely destroy the inputs, not redistribute them.
- **The Grand Forge** (capstone, tier 3) — Ancient Grail (5 Storm Artifact + 1 Crimson Crown → 80 supply), Eternal Diadem (5 Ember Artifact + 1 Void Shard → 40 supply), and Worldheart, a deliberately *branching* capstone (1 Sunforged Core + 1 Voidforged Core → 25 supply) built from two independent sub-ladders of equal depth meeting at the top — the widest recipe in the game on purpose. Capstone input counts are deliberately steep — more of a common than one player could realistically farm alone — on purpose: satisfying one means buying hundreds off the market from everyone else, and that demand is what gives a brand-new player's cheapest starter items any real worth at all. Nothing else in the game does that job.
- **Seasonal/limited events** — a hard end date built directly into config (currently Midsummer Festival, ending August 1 2026, producing Solstice Talisman and Midsummer Wreath). Once an event ends, its items are frozen forever — never craftable, never payable into a roll — a real promise that "limited" means limited, not just "hasn't come back around yet." Seasonal recipes deliberately only ever consume base-pool items so a seasonal recipe can never be gated behind whatever happens to be in rotation that week.
- **Sold-out replacement** — when a permanent recipe's capped output fully sells out, it's automatically swapped for a pre-authored "spare" recipe at the next 20-minute boundary, so a popular recipe running dry doesn't leave a dead, permanently-broken crafting station. If the authored spares run out, the system can generate further fallback variants on the fly (each one asking for slightly more input than the last, output supply shrunk to 80% of the original), specifically so a long-lived station keeps getting harder rather than looping the exact same recipe forever. These generated fallbacks stay hidden from item pickers until one is actually substituted in and crafted, so players don't see a wall of near-identical placeholder rows burying the real item.

A boot-time validator sanity-checks the entire recipe graph and warns on violations: every event-item input must have some recipe that can produce it; no two recipes may ever share the same output (an earlier design had four different commons all feeding one shared "Refined Essence," which read as a bug rather than a feature); a rotating recipe may only produce a terminal item; and an item feeding another recipe may only be time-limited if the thing it feeds is limited on the same schedule.

Consumed crafting inputs are **redistributed back into the shared reserve, not destroyed** (sacrifice recipes are the deliberate exception) — a common eaten by the hundreds at the top of the ladder recycles straight back into new players' starter grants and roll pools instead of the world ever running dry.

**Unique Craft** — a distinct, personal mechanic, separate from the shared ladder. Every player periodically gets temporary access (a 3-hour window) to craft one item that already exists but that no live recipe is currently producing, with a cheap shopping list (2–3 distinct common/rare items, priced at 45–70% of the output's value). The reasoning: everyone chasing the same recipes turns players into each other's competitors instead of trading partners; a Unique Craft drops one player into the market wanting something nobody else is currently bidding on, so whoever happens to hold those parts suddenly has a buyer — which is how a brand-new player who knows nothing about trading makes their first sale. It also incidentally teaches all four ways to get an item (roll, store, board, direct trade) without a word of tutorial, since filling the shopping list can be done any of those ways. Everyone starts with 1 slot; extra slots for holding more than one offer at once are a steep, permanent gem sink: 750 → 2,000 → 5,000 → 12,000 gems, each slot's offer running its own independent expiry and 30-minute cooldown once it lapses. Two slots are guaranteed never to offer the same output at the same time, so it never reads as a duplicate.

### AFK zones

Standing on a pad accrues a reward every 10 minutes, increasing each tick, reset on leaving. The first payout is deliberately a full interval away — AFK income should never beat simply playing normally for ten minutes, only beat leaving the game entirely. The reward ladder (8 rungs) is fully built and wired; every rung currently pays "tbd" (declared, deliberately paying nothing yet, not a bug) — the actual reward type (items, gems, banked trade-ad boosts, or roll credits) hasn't been chosen. Standing too far from the pad (checked every single tick) drops the session — the server never trusts a client's claim to still be AFK. Leaving resets the whole ladder back to the first rung, specifically to stop hopping in and out of a zone to keep re-earning the top payout.

Roblox's own idle-kick timer is a real problem this system has to work around, since Roblox has no API to disable it and it watches real input only. Two fixes were considered and rejected on purpose: simulating input with `VirtualUser` (an undocumented API explicitly scoped to Roblox's own automated testing, not gameplay use), and teleporting the player back into "the same place" periodically (which actually lands them on a *different* server instance without a reserved server slot, resetting the session-length metrics Roblox uses for game discovery and rating — quietly damaging the game's own numbers isn't considered a real fix). The solution instead is honest: the client detects Roblox's own idle signal and nudges the player to press a real on-screen button roughly every 10 minutes, with no automatic save on their behalf.

### The Store

A fixed-price NPC shop, separate from rolls and the one acquisition path with zero randomness. Every item is limited stock at a hand-set gem price that never moves as it sells — 10 items currently, priced 10–25 gems, supplies 25–150. Only a rotating subset (6 of the catalog) is on sale at once, on a 6-hour shelf window that changes deterministically from the clock (every server agrees, no coordination needed), and within that, 2 shelf items go on a genuine, timed 35%-off sale every 15 minutes. That's a real recurring demand event, not decoration: buying below market Value is free profit, so players pile in, that item's stock drains, and it gets genuinely scarcer for everyone else already holding one. Per-player purchase limits apply per item (default: 1, overridable per item), and effective price is always floored at 1 gem so a stacked discount can never make an item free.

## Trading systems

### Direct 1:1 trade

Invite, both sides build an offer (a 3x3 grid, 9 item slots + gems each, up to 99 copies per slot, up to 1,000,000,000 gems), both confirm, a 2-second cancelable grace window (the only artificial wait in the whole flow, deliberately PS99-style — either side can still back out before anything is locked in), then escrowed delivery. Works cross-server and survives a partner disconnecting mid-trade — the authoritative trade record lives in a cross-server store and every state transition is a pure, independently-tested function with hard invariants: changing an offer resets both sides' confirmations (kills last-second swap attempts after agreement), items are only ever locked in once both sides have confirmed, and once settlement begins it cannot be aborted — it's retried until it completes, never left half-done. A house tax can be applied to the gem side of a settled trade (currently set to 0%, after an earlier nonzero rate measurably drained the gem supply faster than the crafting faucet could replace it).

No typed chat required — a canned "quick chat" phrase bar sits right in the trade screen, grouped into four tabs (Offer / Items / Trade / Say) with real trader vocabulary: "That looks fair," "Add more," "That's too much, take some back," "Almost — a little more," "That's way under value" (verdict); "I want your %s," "I'll give you my %s," "Not that one, sorry," "Clean trade? One for one," "What do you want for it?" (items/want); "Ready when you are," "One moment," "Let me think about it," "Yes," "No thanks" (flow); "Hi!," "Thanks!," "Sorry!," "Good luck out there" (social). The item-referencing phrases fill in the actual item name server-side from a slot the client fills, never from raw text. This exists specifically because Roblox restricts text chat between accounts in different age brackets — a 12-year-old and a 15-year-old often literally cannot type to each other — so the whole negotiation is playable by tapping buttons, and a player who can't type can still learn real trading vocabulary by reading the options. Deliberately absent: any win/loss grading on a completed trade.

### Trade board

The biggest system in the game. Public listings with **no escrow at all** — a listing is a promise, not a deposit. Posting only checks you currently hold the goods right now; they stay fully usable, tradeable, and rollable right up until someone actually takes the trade. The real check happens at settlement: if the poster is online on this server, it's an ordinary delta; if offline, it's a safe conditional write directly to their profile; if they're online on a *different* server, the take is refused (their profile is locked there) and the listing just waits to be tried again later; if the poster genuinely can't cover it anymore for any other reason, the listing is cancelled outright rather than serving a broken promise.

Three listing types:
- **Swap** ("this for that") — can auto-settle instantly if the terms match (works even with the poster offline), or register as an "interest" the poster reviews and can invite to trade directly. The want side can hold wildcard tokens instead of naming a specific item — 7 tokens total (Any Offer, Upgrade, Downgrade, Adds, Rares, Demand, Event Item), modeled on Rolimon's request tags, never actual items themselves — which forces ask-first since a fuzzy want can't auto-settle.
- **Bulk** — a standing gem-denominated buy order for N copies of one item (up to 1,000 at once); gems are checked but never held until something actually fills the order.
- **Sell** — a fixed-price gem shop listing; the same mechanism booths use under the hood.

Sell and Bulk orders on the same item cross like a real order book: if a resting sell (ask) and a resting buy order (bid) can match, they fill automatically at the resting order's price — genuine price improvement for whoever takes second, exactly like a continuous double auction.

Player listings live in a cross-server store; the market-bot simulation's own listings (when it's running) live in plain server memory instead, specifically because the shared cross-server store quota is small and experience-wide, and a large bot population holding several quotes each would exceed it outright. A cancelled listing is dropped entirely rather than left to expire naturally, for the same quota reason — leaving stale rows around fills a tiny shared budget in minutes once posting gets frequent.

Listings can be server-scoped (free, dies when you leave that server, no grace at all) or global (cross-server, survives a 2-hour grace window after you disconnect). Everyone gets 3 free ad slots per scope (server and global counted separately — 3 and 3, not 3 total); extra slots are purchasable, capped at 27 total per scope. Paying with Robux boosts placement and duration on a tiered shelf — 1 hour up to 3 days, server or global scope, with a scarce "Featured" tier (only 5 slots per scope, deliberately scarce — a permanent featured pass would let one player squat the pool forever). The shelf pricing is deliberately anchored: a cheap "low" option, an intended "best value" pick, and an expensive "high" option that makes the middle one look reasonable by comparison (e.g. a 24-hour boost costs 2.5x a 1-hour boost for 24x the duration, so the comparison sells itself). An ad's base lifespan and its paid boost duration are two entirely separate clocks — a boost never silently extends how long the underlying ad itself survives, and a boost purchase always banks as a spendable credit rather than applying automatically, so a client can't race the receipt to swap what it applies to. Boosting never gates the ability to post, browse, or accept trades for free — money only ever buys position and duration.

An ongoing presence sweep re-confirms an ad's poster is still around and keeps a server-scoped ad's clock alive without a full re-post, and can auto-renew a Featured ad from a banked credit if a slot is free when the current one lapses.

Visually, the board reads as a scrolling feed with a **gold-bordered "Featured" strip** pinned above it (roughly 3 cards visible per page, scrolling to fit up to 5), each featured card rendered exactly like a normal feed row just boxed in gold so it never feels like a second, different UI. Regular boosted (non-featured) ads get an **electric-cyan** tag instead — gold is reserved exclusively for Featured so the two tiers never read as the same thing at a glance. Putting up more than one copy of an item in a single trade offer doesn't create duplicate slots — it collapses into one square with a large impact-font count badge ("x5"), and any stack a player holds more than one of gets a small green "+" in the corner of its picker tile that opens a one-tap bulk-add prompt instead of clicking the item repeatedly. Accepting a trade shows an explicit "Are you sure?" confirmation screen before it fires. A live activity ticker shows recent board fills (deliberately illustrative, rate-limited to a handful of announcements per second — not meant as an audit log).

### Booths

Hand-placed player shops in the world, free to claim (hold E). A booth doesn't sell anything of its own — stocking a slot just posts an ordinary Sell listing on the board, so the exact same stock shows on the booth *and* the board at one price, and nothing is ever held twice. Base 5 slots, purchasable up to 20, arranged in a 5x2 visible grid (scrolls for more). No purchase cooldown, deliberately: unlike games where a booth slot is a mutable display a seller can swap between a buyer reading it and clicking, here the listing ID *is* the offer — price and item are immutable once posted, so a seller who changes their mind can only cancel, which just makes a pending buy fail with "gone" and charges the buyer nothing. A cooldown would protect against nothing and only punish honest buyers. Players standing within 12 studs of a booth render semi-transparent, so nobody can body-block a stall and hide its stock from other shoppers. A claim itself is server-local and purely in-memory (not written to the cross-server store at all) — booths are high-churn, low-durability state that would burn through the shared quota for no real benefit, and the map's own player cap is set to match the number of placed booths so one is always available. Cosmetic booth skins are purchasable (5 currently, placeholder recolors pending real art, applied by recoloring specific named parts on the model) but never touch price, tax, or slot count — kept strictly cosmetic on purpose so a storefront upgrade can never become pay-to-win.

## Scam safeguards

- **Direct trades are fully escrowed with hard state-machine invariants** — changing an offer resets both sides' confirmations, confirming is only legal during open negotiation, and both items are locked in before either side is credited. Settlement, once started, can't be aborted and is retried until it completes, never left half-done — nobody can accept a trade and simply not pay.
- **Booth and board listings can't be swapped out from under a buyer.** The listing itself *is* the offer — price and item are immutable the moment it's posted, so there's no window where what you saw and what you get can differ.
- **Players standing near a booth go semi-transparent**, so nobody can body-block a stall and hide its stock from other shoppers.
- **The trade board's no-escrow design is safe by construction, not by trust**: a listing take either succeeds atomically or fails and charges nobody — there's no window where one side pays and the other doesn't.
- **Group chat text is filtered per reader**, not pre-rendered once and broadcast — the same string can legitimately read differently (which words get blocked varies by viewer) per person reading it, so it's filtered fresh for every single reader rather than once at the source.
- **Trade quick-chat carries zero player-authored text** — only phrase IDs cross the network, and the server looks up the actual wording — so there is nothing for an exploiter to inject and nothing that needs filtering in the first place.

## Chat systems

Two entirely separate systems, deliberately not unified:

- **Trade quick-chat** — canned phrases only (covered above under Direct Trade). No player-authored text ever crosses the network.
- **Syndicate/group chat** — real typed text, for members of the same syndicate. Every message is filtered through Roblox's moderation *per reader*, capped at 140 characters. Deliberately **not stored durably** — chat is high-frequency and a DataStore write costs roughly half a second, so persisting every line would be both slow and needlessly expensive for something nobody reads twice. Messages are delivered cross-server for the live case, and each server keeps a small rolling buffer (the last 40 messages) so a player opening the chat panel sees recent context; anything older than that is deliberately gone rather than kept around.

## Progression & social systems

**Syndicates** (guilds) — founded for 500 gems, fully custom roles and permissions (owner can create/rename/reorder/delete up to 8 roles, not a hardcoded preset list — permissions like invite/recruit/kick/promote/demote/manage-roles/disband are assigned per role; only Owner and a base Member role are permanent and can't be deleted). Join policy is configurable per syndicate: request-to-join, fully open, or invite-only. XP comes from things players already do — 10 XP per trade (both sides earn it), 6 per board fill, 15 per craft (the highest, deliberately, since crafting is the actual value-creating action in the economy). A donation/request system is explicitly modeled on Clash of Clans' troop-donation mechanic — described in the code itself as "the single strongest retention mechanic in that game" — where members post requests (up to 2 open at once per member, capped at 500 total per syndicate, expiring after 24 hours) and others donate items to fill them for 12 XP each, more than a completed trade earns. 10 levels, cumulative XP up to 75,000, unlocking a rising member cap (10 → 15 → 20 → 25 → 30 → 40 → 50 → 60 → 75 → 100) and stacking perks: extra board slots (+1 at level 2, +2 more at level 5, +3 more at level 8), a market fee discount (5% at level 3, 10% at level 6, 15% at level 9), and a roll-luck bonus (+0.02 at level 4, +0.05 at level 7, +0.08 at level 10). Six communal syndicate goals exist on top of leveling (from 25 total trades for 400 XP up to 250 trades for 2,500 XP, and similarly for crafts and donations), each paying out once.

**Leaderboards** — Portfolio Value (live net worth), Total Trades, and Rarest Item Held (scored inversely by supply — the scarcer the item you hold, the higher the score), each with all-time and auto-resetting weekly variants. Top 50 shown per board. Weekly boards need no reset job at all — the store name itself is derived from the current calendar week, so when the week turns over the name simply stops matching and a fresh empty board starts on its own; old weeks stay readable under their own name. Scores are pushed on a staggered per-player timer (currently every 3 minutes per player, deliberately not per-event, since portfolio value can change hundreds of times a minute during active trading and pushing on every change would blow the leaderboard store's write budget), but a player's own live value is overlaid on top of the cached board the instant they open it, so a just-completed trade is never invisible on their own screen even if the board hasn't caught up yet for everyone else.

**Milestones & communal goals** — a personal craft-count reward track (5 crafts → 1,200 crafts, "Apprentice" through "Legend," rewarding items, never gems, since gems only ever enter the game through ads or Robux and paying them out here would quietly turn crafting into a second gem faucet) and a daily server-wide craft-count goal (400 crafts by anyone combined, contributors-only claim so it can't be farmed by someone who did nothing). Both fully built, both currently switched off pending enabling and reward tuning.

**Referrals & ad rewards** — invite 3 friends through Roblox's own game-invite prompt (a friend only ever counts once, only on their genuinely first-ever join, so leaving and rejoining can never re-credit an invite) and both the inviter and everyone they brought get a 20% gem bonus on their next 15 rewarded-ad watches. Rewarded ad video itself is gated behind Roblox's own platform requirement (a public game, run by a 13+ ID-verified creator, with 2,000+ monthly unique visitors) — shown to players honestly as a visible progress bar toward that number rather than hidden away until it's ready. Once unlocked: 50 gems per watch, capped at 5 watches a day, with a 2-minute cooldown between watches.

## Monetization

Config-driven, all in one place, structured so adding a new offer is a data edit, not new code. Gem packs (5 tiers, 100 → 6,000 gems, with an escalating bonus of 0% → 40% at the top) use deliberate pricing psychology — the middle pack is flagged "Most Popular" as a decoy anchor pulling buyers up the ladder, and the top pack is the featured hero card meant to anchor the whole shelf from above. Gamepasses include an "Always Boosted" pass and an "Ad Duration" pass (ads survive a full day even while offline) — both deliberately *excluded* from the Featured board tier, since a permanent featured pass would let one player squat the scarce Featured pool forever. Ad slots stack additively (+1/+3/+5). Booth slots and skins are purchasable but cosmetic-only. Trade-ad boosting is covered above. Across all of it: money consistently buys visibility, duration, convenience, and cosmetics — never an advantage in the trading/economy math itself, and never blocks the free path to post, browse, or accept trades.

Every real-money purchase in the game — gems, gamepasses, subscriptions, ad-slot bundles, booth cosmetics, ad-reward payouts — routes through one single purchase handler, since Roblox only allows a game to register one at all; every grant is idempotent by construction so a replayed receipt (which Roblox can and does occasionally deliver more than once) can never pay out twice.

There's also a parallel, currently-empty system for *tradeable* purchase tokens — since a Roblox gamepass itself can't be resold or traded, any future benefit meant to be player-tradeable would be granted as an ordinary inventory item instead, with gameplay perks keyed off holding that item rather than off Roblox's own gamepass-ownership API. Infrastructure exists; nothing uses it yet.

### Community Rewards — the actual reason gems only ever enter through spending

**This is a foundational design intent, not built yet.** It's the reason the economy enforces, everywhere, that gems can only ever be minted by a real Robux purchase or a rewarded-ad watch — never by any in-game action, never as a gameplay reward. That invariant exists specifically to support this system safely.

The intended mechanism: gems are sold to players at one rate (for example, 1 Robux buys 10 gems) but only redeemable back at a deliberately much worse rate (for example, 100 gems would be needed to redeem 1 Robux back) — a 10x spread in the house's favor. Because gems can never enter the economy any other way, and the redemption rate is always worse than the purchase rate, it is *mathematically impossible* for the total value ever redeemed to exceed the total value ever actually paid in by real players. The game cannot lose money on this system by construction, regardless of how the in-game economy itself moves around internally.

This is **not** an automated in-game cashout button, and deliberately can't be — Roblox's Terms of Use prohibit redeeming Robux for real currency outside the developer's own DevEx program, and prohibit exchanging virtual currency for real money more broadly (see below). Instead, the intended flow is a manual, human-reviewed process: once a player crosses a gem threshold, they can send a request to the developers; the developers check it against eligibility criteria (presumably things like account standing, legitimate play history, and anti-abuse checks — not yet specified); and if approved, the reward is paid out as real Robux via Roblox's **Group Payout** feature — a standard, legitimate Roblox mechanism for a group to distribute its own earned funds to group members at the group owners' discretion. This is fundamentally a discretionary community-reward/prize program funded entirely by real purchases and paid out manually by a human, not an automated redemption of virtual currency — which is the distinction that keeps it on the right side of Roblox's policy. Comparable to how some other Roblox games run manually-reviewed leaderboard or contest prize payouts through their group's Robux balance.

Open, unresolved design questions worth settling before this is built: what the gem threshold to qualify should be, what the eligibility criteria actually are, how requests get reviewed at scale without it becoming a manual bottleneck as the player base grows, and how the exchange-rate spread should be tuned so it's generous enough to feel like a real reward worth chasing while still comfortably guaranteeing the game never operates at a loss.

## Items & rarity

Six rarity tiers (Common → Rare → Legendary → Mythical → Godly → Celestial), derived automatically from each item's total supply cap — never hand-labeled. Thresholds: Common ≥20,000 copies ever mintable, Rare ≥5,000, Legendary ≥1,200, Mythical ≥300, Godly ≥80, Celestial below that (down to about 40). The top three tiers are deliberately kept close together in supply so the value ladder has no big cliff between them. Base store price roughly doubles per tier: 8 / 35 / 130 / 300 / 650 / 1,300 gems for Common through Celestial — a smooth ladder specifically so there's no price gap a roll price could fall into and accidentally force a jackpot-only pool.

An item's default draw weight (how often it's picked within its rarity) is derived sublinearly from its supply cap — roughly `supply^0.75` — so a rarer item draws less often than a common one, but never effectively zero; a raw linear weighting would make the rarest items nearly unreachable within their own tier.

New players get a starter bundle of 5 weighted draws (duplicates allowed) rather than a fixed kit. That's deliberate asymmetry, not noise: because it's weighted rather than uniform, two new players end up with meaningfully different portfolios — one got luckier than the other — which means there's immediately something worth trading between them on day one, instead of everyone starting with an identical kit nobody wants to swap.

Beyond the rotating 5-item starter pool, dozens more items exist purely as crafting/event outputs, each with its own independent supply cap. Procedurally generated pools build item names from a pool of 26 adjectives and 26 nouns (e.g. "Ancient Totem," "Shadow Blade"), retrying on collision, and draw icons from a shared 25-image pool that prefers icons not already in use elsewhere before recycling — so a fresh pool's items look and sound distinct from what already exists as long as there's room to.

## Interface conventions

Large numbers are always abbreviated in the UI (1,300 gems reads "1.3K," not the raw number, using one shared formatter everywhere so the same number never reads differently on two screens), and any numeric input box accepts shorthand typing — typing "3k" into a quantity or price field resolves to 3000 automatically. Destructive admin actions (full economy reset) require 3 separate clicks spaced at least 3 seconds apart within a 12-second window, rather than a single confirm dialog, specifically to make it hard to trigger by accident. Every recipe row shows a "How to make" button opening a recipe-chain view, so a player missing an ingredient can see exactly where it comes from rather than staring at a locked recipe with no path forward — and if that ingredient is itself a rotating-recipe output not active this window, the game says so honestly and gives the actual time until it rotates back in, rather than pointing at a station that's currently closed.

## Other systems

**Player profiles** — a browsable public page for every player, viewable whether they're online, offline, or on a different server entirely, built around one deliberate rule: privacy is enforced by *omission at write time*, not by filtering at read time. Hidden sections are never written into the public record in the first place, so there's no code path that could accidentally leak them later.

What a profile shows:
- **Showcase** — up to 6 items on display. If the player hasn't picked any, it defaults to their 6 scarcest owned items (fewest copies in existence, ties broken deterministically) — so an empty profile still shows off something interesting rather than nothing. Only items still actually owned resolve; selling a showcased item quietly drops it from view.
- **Stat chips** — up to 3, chosen from a menu of 9: Portfolio Value, Trades, Gems Earned, Mythical+ Owned, Celestials Owned, Distinct Items, Items Crafted, Friends Invited, Gems. Defaults to Portfolio Value / Trades / Mythical+ Owned if never customized. "Mythical+ Owned" deliberately counts Mythical *and* every tier above it (Godly, Celestial) rather than just Mythical alone — the code notes this was a real gap once Godly and Celestial were added above Mythical and the chip briefly undercounted a player's two rarest tiers; "Celestials Owned" exists separately for players who specifically want to flex the single top tier on its own.
- **A status line** — up to 60 characters, filtered through Roblox's text moderation before it's ever stored.
- **Portfolio Value** itself, shown as a headline number unless hidden.
- **Two independent privacy toggles**: hide portfolio value, hide showcase. Either can be off while the other stays on.

Profile reads are deliberately lock-free — no session lock, just a plain cached read — specifically so an offline or cross-server player's profile stays viewable instead of erroring. Writes are debounced (4 seconds after an inventory or gem change) since trading can mutate a player's inventory many times a minute and writing the public snapshot on every single change would be wasteful.

**Notifications** — deliberately offline-only; a player currently in the game gets nothing from this system (the live UI already shows current state, and toasting every board event was judged noise). An offline player gets a durable queued notification (up to 20 kept, oldest dropped first) replayed at next login, plus a real push notification through Roblox's own notification API for opted-in players (subject to Roblox's hard platform limit of one push per player per day per experience).

## Built for ongoing content — the live-ops backbone

The crafting/event system was deliberately architected as an engine for a constant stream of updates, not a one-time content drop:

- **Seasonal/limited events** are a first-class category with a hard end date baked into config (add a new event, give it recipes and an end timestamp, done — no code changes). Once an event ends, its items are frozen forever, which is what makes each season feel genuinely limited-time rather than always-repeatable.
- **Rotating recipes** cycle a subset of a larger recipe pool on a fixed timer (currently 4 of a pool of 6, every 10 minutes) with zero stored state — purely a deterministic function of the clock, so every server agrees automatically and there's nothing to break on rotation.
- **Sold-out replacement** — when a permanent recipe's capped output sells out, it's automatically swapped for a pre-authored "spare" recipe at the next boundary, so a popular recipe going dry doesn't leave a dead station; new ones can just be authored and dropped in.
- New items, new recipes, and new seasonal events are all pure config additions on top of this — the intent is that content updates are mostly data, not new code.

## Built but currently switched off (ready to launch)

A few systems are fully implemented and wired end-to-end but intentionally disabled pending a decision or more polish:

- **Milestones & communal goals** — a personal craft-count reward track (5 → 1,200 crafts, "Apprentice" through "Legend") and a daily server-wide craft-count goal (400 crafts by anyone, contributors-only claim). Both are considered ready to launch — the mechanism itself isn't in question, only the specific reward items and quantities at each tier still need tuning before flipping `enabled = true`. Not blocked on any larger design decision; this is pure numbers-balancing work.
- **AFK zone reward payout** — the AFK system itself (zones, capacity, freeze/unfreeze, the escalating 8-rung payout schedule, idle-kick handling) is fully built and wired end-to-end; every rung currently pays "tbd" on purpose. The decided direction: **items, primarily**, drawn from the AFK ladder's own reward slot — with room to expand beyond pure items later (the granter registry already supports gems, banked trade-ad boosts, and roll credits as additional reward kinds per rung, so a later rung mixing in something beyond items is a config change, not new code). What's still open is which specific items back each rung and how the escalation curve should scale value across the 8 rungs — that's a balancing pass, not an architecture question.
- **Real Robux product IDs** — gem packs and some ad-slot dev products are still on placeholder IDs pending the store going live.

## Open idea: an action-based tutorial for a guaranteed first profit

**Decided direction, not just a research question:** the intent is an explicit tutorial/quest flow — not a wall of text, an *action* tutorial that teaches by having the new player actually do the thing. It should walk them through one genuinely profitable trade end-to-end, and in the process introduce most of the game's other systems along the way, so the tutorial doubles as the new-player's first real tour of the game rather than a separate wall of instructions bolted on before it.

What already exists that this tutorial would be built on top of, rather than invented from scratch:

- **The starter bundle is deliberately asymmetric** (5 weighted draws, not a fixed kit), specifically so two new players land on different portfolios and have something worth trading with each other on day one — the tutorial's first trade opportunity already exists the moment a player spawns in, without any special-casing.
- **Unique Craft** hands a new player a cheap, concrete shopping list (2–3 common/rare items) priced well below what the output is worth, with nobody else in the market currently competing for those same parts — mechanically, this already *is* a guaranteed-profitable trade. A tutorial doesn't need to invent a scripted "free win"; it can point the player at their real, live Unique Craft offer and walk them through actually filling it.
- **The Alchemist's Lab** is the one recipe station that's always cheap and always on, by design, as a no-fail on-ramp — a safe second stop once the tutorial has taught rolling/trading, since it can't ever be closed by rotation.
- **The Store's timed 35%-off sales** are, mechanically, free profit if bought and held — a natural first flip that needs no trading skill at all, just knowing to look, and a good candidate for an early tutorial beat since it's a single NPC interaction rather than a negotiation.

A concrete shape this could take, action-by-action rather than as a text wall: spawn with the starter bundle → tutorial points at a Unique Craft offer already sitting on the player's own profile and explains *why* it's worth doing (the shopping list costs less than the output is worth) → sends them to the Store or the Board to actually acquire the missing input → has them complete the craft → has them list or trade the resulting profit, closing the loop with a real, player-understood "I turned X into more than X." Each step is a real interaction with a real system (Store, Board or direct trade, Crafting), so by the end the player has touched most of the core loop once, on purpose, with a concrete reason for each step rather than an arbitrary checklist.

What's still open and worth researching against comparable games:
- What's the right number of steps/systems to chain in one first-session tutorial before it stops feeling like guided play and starts feeling like a chore? Where do other Roblox trading/collector games draw that line?
- Should the tutorial reward be exactly what the underlying Unique Craft offer would have paid out anyway (fully authentic, no special-casing), or is a guaranteed floor worth adding so a tutorial can never fail to end in a genuine profit regardless of what the player's specific live offer happened to be?
- Is there a risk that a guaranteed-profit tutorial teaches the wrong lesson (that trading is a solved puzzle rather than a live market), and if so, how do other games frame their first guided win to avoid that?

## Open idea: bots as a new-player liquidity backstop

Genuinely undecided whether this ships at all — not a committed direction, purely an idea being weighed. A full bot-driven market simulation already exists in the codebase — hundreds of agents with distinct trading personalities (value-driven buyers, trend chasers, hoarders, crafters, collectors, and pure market-makers who quote both sides purely for liquidity) — but it's currently switched off while the economy is tuned directly against real player behavior rather than simulated behavior. Reviving that exact full simulation is *not* what's being considered here.

If this does get built, it's currently framed as two genuinely different shapes, not variations on one idea:

- **One bot with disproportionately large resources.** A single, deep-pocketed counterparty always available to trade against — simple to reason about and cheap to run, but it means every new player's first trade is against the *same* one entity every time, which may read as artificial precisely because it's singular and always there.
- **Many smaller bots, purely for starter-player liquidity.** A population of lightweight agents whose only job is making sure a new player always has *someone* real-feeling to trade with — closer in spirit to the existing simulation's "maker" archetype (which exists specifically to quote both sides for liquidity, not to express any directional market opinion), but scoped down and re-purposed specifically for the new-player cold-start problem rather than simulating the whole economy.

Both shapes solve the same underlying problem: a brand-new player's first session having nobody to trade with, especially on a quiet server. Worth researching before committing to either: how comparable trading games solve this cold-start problem, and whether a single large counterparty or a distributed population of small ones reads as more genuine to a new player who doesn't yet know which one they're looking at.
