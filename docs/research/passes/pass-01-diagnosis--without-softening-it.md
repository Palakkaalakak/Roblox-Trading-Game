Diagnosis, without softening it
What you have described is a correct economy wrapped around a missing verb.

The starter pools, the asymmetric hands, the unique craft, the split-and-combine crafter, the board, the booth, the bulk order, the rank gates on features rather than on currency, the overhead title, the crew, the solvency rule on gems — those are the right organs. They are not connected by a single conversion, so in the first ten minutes the player experiences a tour. Tour games bounce.

The specific leaks:

The verb is “trade,” and trading is a matching problem. A new player does not know what anything is worth, may not have a counterparty, and has no solo action that feels like winning while they wait. A liquidity bot that fills trades removes the emptiness and does not create desire. Desire is a hole the player can see. A bot is plumbing.

Asymmetry is invisible unless the first screen shows the hole, not just the gift. “You received 14 items” is a loot shower. “You are loaded with Shards and you cannot finish the thing your hand is for until you get a Core, and that booth has a Core” is a loop.

The unique craft, if it is a one-time trophy, dies in the tutorial. Asymmetry has to regenerate every session or day-8 players are all holding similar books and the market becomes a swap meet of leftovers.

The roll, the gem-store rotation, quests, AFK pads, and crews are currently adjacent products. If a player can describe them as separate tabs, they are separate tabs. They will feel like chores by day 3 and they will not move RFY’s week-3 signals.

The Robux vault cannot be the minute-to-minute reward, and the math is why. Your own rule: gems enter at 10 per 1 Robux that reaches you, via purchase or ads, and nowhere else. Cashout at 100 gems per 1 Robux is a 10-to-1 spread. Playing does not mint gems. So “earn Robux” is not a core loop you can deliver every thirty seconds without breaking solvency. It is a north star. If you let players measure progress in gems, non-payers have no progress, payers progress by paying, and the game is a cashier. The number that play moves has to be an item-denominated book: craft value and realized spread. Gems are how impatience and cashout are priced. Profit titles are how play is scored. Those are different ladders, and they must stay different or the design collapses into either insolvency or a paywall.

The Blox Fruits instinct — a second way to get items — is the right instinct and the wrong implementation if the second way is combat with its own power curve. Two verbs in the first week is how trading games die. A source of inputs that only matter because the floor wants them is not a second game. It is the faucet.

The verb
Close a spread.

Every item has two numbers the game itself maintains, so nobody needs a wiki.

Craft value: what the recipe graph says the item costs in base units. Combine and split make this number objective. You author it.
Last sale: what the floor just paid. The players author it. The bot’s market-making prints early sales so the number exists on day one.
The spread is the gap. Your book value is the sum of craft values you hold, marked against last sale where that helps the fantasy. A flip is any action that turns a worse spread into a better one: crafting into a recipe the floor is paying above craft value, splitting a fat item into units the floor is short on, rolling a dead item into a live tag, buying a booth listing under craft value, filling someone else’s hole at a premium. The overhead title tracks realized spread, not gems. The player always knows if the last thirty seconds won.

That is limiteds, made legible, and made solo-playable. Limiteds traders already play this game against RAP versus believed value. You are just putting both numbers on the item so a new player can play it before they are initiated.

Craft, roll, and deal are not three features. They are three speeds of closing a spread.

Craft is the always-on speed. No counterparty. Deterministic when the recipe is known. This is the heartbeat that saves the first session and every dead hour.
Roll is craft with a wider band, a sacrifice, and a timer. Same items in, same book out. The uncertainty is higher, so the juice is louder.
Deal is the social speed, where the spread can jump further than any recipe, and where other people watch.
If a system does not take items from this graph and put items back on this graph, it does not ship.

The signature, which is what makes asymmetry permanent
The starter hand is necessary and not sufficient. Pools that deal some players a few high items and others many low items create the first hour of demand. Then crafting equalizes everyone, unless each player has a persistent bias.

The unique craft should not be a consumable trophy. It should install a signature: a permanent personal modifier, revealed on the takeover screen, that biases every signed craft toward one tag family and away from another. You were dealt few Cores; your signature crafts efficiently when a Core is an input and inefficiently when you feed it the Shards you have piles of. So the game’s first lesson, taught by the hand and the signature together, is: my waste is someone else’s input, and my hole does not go away.

Signed outputs carry your tag bias. Player A’s efficient output is statistically what Player B’s signature is hungry for, because signatures are dealt from opposing pools. You do not need the bot to pretend to want things. The graph wants them. The bot’s job is temporal market-making: it holds inventory, posts at craft value plus a small edge, and rebalances toward tags the current server is short on, so a player is never stuck with zero counterparties at 2am. It should not gift value. It should warehouse the other side of the spread. That is liquidity. Generosity would inflate craft value into meaninglessness, and believed scarcity would die.

The guaranteed first unique is the moment the signature is installed. They combine the tutorial inputs, the result comes out stamped, the stamp is visibly theirs, and the UI shows the hole the stamp just created. IKEA plus endowment plus Zeigarnik in one animation. Then you never consume the signature. You feed it forever. That is the day-8–28 personal hole.

The first two minutes, as one cycle
Spawn is on the floor, not in a menu. Overhead title already says the first rank, so identity exists before achievement. The takeover is a hand of cards, not an inventory dump. It ends on a single sentence the game has earned the right to say: you are heavy on Shards, your signature wants Cores, one recipe is one input away, and that input is pulsing on a booth fifteen studs from you.

They walk. Walking is the tutorial. The crafter is a station, not a dialog tree. They feed the dealt items, the signature stamps, the result hits with the full juice (this is the public sound the server will learn means “someone just made something”), book value ticks, and the title bar — already endowed, first title placed close on purpose, Kivetz head start without lying about the later ladder — jumps.

Now they are holding something their signature does not want to keep. That is the investment. The pulsing booth will buy it, or the missing Core is standing there as a split the bot posted. One deal. Book value moves again. The board option appears as an echo, not a lesson: “leave it listed, you will get pinged.” That listing is the external open loop.

Before two minutes they have also seen one roll machine eat a dead item and spit a near-miss (“one tag off a recipe you don’t know yet”), so the second speed of the verb is felt, not explained. They log out, if they log out, with a listing live, a roll cooling down, a title incomplete, and a signature they now understand as theirs. That is rows 2, 4, and 5 cleared in a single scene. Your current tutorial ends at the crafter and then releases them into a feature list. Do not do that. The release is the walk to the booth. The game starts when the hole is visible. It does not start when the menus unlock.

Rank gates stay, and they gate stage, not the verb. Noob can craft, sign, roll, and deal 1:1 on the starter floor, because those are the verb. Casual opens the board so deals persist offline. Regular opens a personal booth, which is the verb performed as status. Trader opens gem listings, bulk, and the vault. Gating the verb behind quests is how you create the bounce RFY now punishes. Gating leverage behind quests is how you make the next permission feel like a new intensity of the same game.

Quests are the loop narrated, or they are junk. “Sign three crafts. Sell one item your signature doesn’t want. Fill one hole.” Daily is the same sentence with today’s tag. Weekly is the same sentence summed across the crew. A quest that sends them to an NPC to read lore is a leak. A quest skip product is only worth selling once the quest is the loop and the skip is “complete this craft now while the buyer is here.”

How the rest of your feature list becomes organs
Crafter. Combine is the spread-closer when the floor pays above craft value for the output. Split is the liquidity action: a high item becomes many low items the starter hands are short on. Experimental craft, using unknown combinations, is the hidden-recipe layer. Known recipes are the price language. Unknown recipes are the mastery layer and the near-miss layer. A near-miss states how close the tags were, so the failure points at the next try. Discovering a recipe is competence they will overvalue (IKEA), and it can stay private as edge or be performed as status. A “Recipe Hunter” title on the overhead is a path-linked envy object. Do not publish the full recipe book. A fully known graph is a spreadsheet, and spreadsheets are not compulsive. A graph they are still mapping is unpredictability tied to agency.

Roll. It consumes an item, never a free pull. Sacrifice is what makes the outcome feel owned rather than dispensed. What they feed shifts the band — feed a Core, the band leans toward Core-adjacent tags. That is the illusion of control, and it is also true enough that veterans can talk about “how to feed.” Gems, or an ad priced at your 10:1 rate, buy a timer skip or a second input that narrows the band. They do not buy a guaranteed win. Guaranteed wins kill the prediction error and teach the payer that play is the stupid version of paying. The machine is on the floor. Hits are public. The screenshot is the item, the feed, and the overhead title. Cooldown is the appointment. Missing a cooldown matters more if the store rotation and the roll share a clock, so the player who returns for one is already inside the other.

Gem store rotation. It stocks tags, not random cool stuff. The rotation is interesting only when it sometimes contains the player’s current hole, and they know the window will close. A subscription whose actual effect is “the rotation is biased toward your signature’s hole” is the correct subscription, because canceling it feels like losing a personal advantage (loss aversion on a routine), and the advantage is the loop. Discounts anchor against craft value, not against a fake original price. If the store is the best way to get every tag whenever you want, you have deleted scarcity and you have deleted the reason to trade. The store is a sometimes-faucet for impatience. The floor is the reliable faucet.

Board, booth, 1:1, bulk. One sale, four frictions. 1:1 is the high-juice, high-agency version: negotiation, with craft value and last sale both visible, so the argument has a language. Frame the confirm as what they keep, not only what they give up, unless you want them to refuse — loss framing on the confirm button will suppress closes, which you do not want on the core action. Use loss framing on the cancel and on the logout (“this offer dies in 20 minutes”). The booth is the stage and the AFK-compatible version: your waste displayed on your body-adjacent stall, other players shopping you, your title overhead. The board is the async version so the loop runs while they are at school; a filled offer is the external trigger, and it is a legitimate notification. Bulk is the best social idea in your list if it is a split made public: a high-tier player splits a fat item into units, many small players reserve units with items or with gems. Item-side fills are play. Gem-side fills are a sink. Same order. That is intentional co-play with a reason, which is the RFY signal, and it is also how a noob touches a high item without you minting one for them.

Crews. A crew is a shared book, not a chat with perks. The weekly bar is collective spread closed, or collective holes filled, using each other’s waste. Members see who has not dumped their mismatched items into the crew book. Your name on a stalled bar is the social cost. The perk is a crew booth row or a crew signature bias on a weekly craft, never gems. A perk that prints currency violates your solvency rule and also skips the verb. Inviting a friend matters because two opposing signatures in one crew fill each other faster than either fills on the public floor. That is the invite reward. It is also co-play.

AFK pads. Sitting there keeps your booth listed and charges your roll cooldown or drips a recipe hint (information, not an item). You are a liquidity provider who happens to be idle, which matches the fantasy and keeps the server looking alive. The pad does not mint items and does not mint gems. Minting items from nothing inflates the graph and kills believed scarcity, which kills the whole game. If you want the pad to occasionally yield a low item, it has to come out of the bot’s warehouse or a system sink, a recycle, not a print. And you do not design around the 60-minute signal cap. Qualified return sessions do the distribution work. The pad just means they left a body on the floor.

Leaderboards. Server book-value, weekly spread gained, hottest flip this hour. The hottest flip is a recipe advertisement and a path: they can see what was fed and what came out. A global all-time richest board can exist for the vault-chasers. It cannot be the board a new player sees first.

Ranks and profit titles. Rank is permission. Title is position. Both float. Titles are a long ladder of realized spread so there is always a next stamp in the goal-gradient zone. The first three titles are close. The later ones stretch. Never let the visible next title sit at 0% when the previous one pops; reveal it already started, because the previous title’s last deals counted toward the family. That is endowed progress you can justify in fiction as “the floor already knows your name.”

The vault. Show it on day one as a locked object with a number, Trader-gated, so it works as curiosity and epic meaning without becoming the verb. The bar fills with gems, and gems still only enter through purchases and ads at 10 per Robux received. Playing fills the title ladder. Paying and watching fill the vault. When a Trader converts, the conversion is public — a server-wide hit, a title change — because that is the proof moment for the north star, and it recruits the next chaser. Do not put this in the title or the icon. The icon shows a booth, a stamped item, a number jumping. The first minute shows the same thing. The vault is discovered inside, which is both the better fantasy and the version discovery will not punish.

Sourcing, the “second part,” folded in. Add it. Do not add a combat game.

A sourcing run is a short physical leave from the floor that returns you with tagged inputs, and the inputs are worthless except as spread. Three forms, all the same verb:

The map has nodes, fruit-style, that drop tag shards and torn recipes. Finding one is the Blox Fruits pleasure — a hunt, a rare identity object, a story. The object does not make you punch harder. It makes your next craft or your next deal different. You come back to the floor to realize it. If you let the shard equip as power, you have started a second game, and the traders and the fighters will split your population until neither side fills the other’s book.

On a timer, a distressed stall cracks on the floor itself. First bodies there haul components. This is appointment plus public scarcity plus co-play, and it happens where the game already is, so you do not split the server into a dungeon population and a market population. The components are tags the current server is short on, rolled from the same shortage the bot is tracking. The event is the economy, dramatized.

Occasionally a market boss: a seller who will only release a fat item if the server, or a crew, assembles a recipe in a window. The fat item cannot be useful to everyone. It has to be split and dealt, or the event mints a winner and a crowd of spectators who gained nothing. Spectators who gained nothing do not come to the next one. Design the payout as components and a split, so twenty players leave with a reason to trade each other in the next ten minutes. That after-event trading spike is the retention. The boss is the trailer.

This is the answer to the fear that trading-plus-bot is not a first-hour loop. The first hour is craft and signature, which need no one. The first day is deals against the bot and whoever is awake. The first week is sourcing and other players, once the price language is in their hands. You do not delay the social game behind a combat tutorial. You give the solo speed of the verb immediately, and you let the social speed become the better payout once the server can support it. The bot bridges the gap and then gets priced out by real players, because real players pay above craft value for the exact tag they need and the bot should not.

Monetization inside this loop, under your gem rule
Gems never drop from play, quests, crews, pads, or events. Every gem in a player’s balance corresponds to Robux that reached you, at 10 gems per Robux, from a pack or from an ad. Item rewards, recipe hints, cooldown charge, booth time, and title progress are the play rewards. They are not free money. They are position.

The moments that sell:

The hole is on screen, the rotation has that tag, the timer is visible. Gem price, with the pack anchor sitting behind it, and an ad button that grants the gem-priced action only if the ad revenue covers it at rate. Rate-limit ads so the floor is not an ad player.
The roll is one minute from ready and a buyer is at the booth. Dev product skips the roll timer or adds the narrowing input.
The board post is sitting under fresher posts. A boost pass or a consumable bumps it. This is pay-to-be-seen, and on a trading floor that is pay-to-play the social speed, which spenders will buy and non-spenders can still route around via 1:1 and crew.
Extra booth slot as a pass. More of their waste on stage, more fills, more status. Permanent leverage, correct as a pass.
The subscription is the personalized rotation bias plus a second listed offer. Canceling costs a felt personal edge.
Do not sell a better signature. The signature is the fairness fiction that keeps non-payers inside the graph. If paying changes what your hand is for, non-payers are content for payers, they feel it, and week-3 play days collapse. Paying changes speed, surface area, and impatience. Playing changes position. The vault is the only place paying converts directly into the north star, and the 10-to-1 spread is the house. Show the vault. Do not show the spread math in a way that makes the vault the thing they think they are grinding toward with crafts, or the first player who does the division will tell the server, and the server will believe them.

What “success” means for this specific game
The population splits, and the loop is built so each split feeds the others. New players craft and sign, produce waste, and buy splits. Mid players hunt recipes, run booths, and source. High players split fat items into bulk, post the gem market, and chase the vault. The bot stabilizes early and overnight. Crews internalize the waste. None of these are modes. They are positions on one graph. A new player and a Trader need each other for the same reason your starter pools exist: their books are wrong in opposite directions. Your job is to keep minting that wrongness faster than crafting erases it. The signature is what does that permanently. Sourcing events do it in bursts. Rotating store stock does it for payers. If you ever let all three stop, the economy equilibrates, spreads go to zero, and the verb dies. A dead spread is a dead game, no matter how many tabs you add.

Live ops, for the day 8–28 window and beyond, is new tags and new recipes on a cadence, not new menus. A new tag family makes every old signature slightly wrong again. That is a seasonal reset of the chase that does not wipe the book, so sunk cost survives and novelty returns. Wiping books would cash out the endowment and they would leave. Adding a tag reopens the hole inside a book they already overvalue. That is the correct seasonal loop.

The build order that keeps it one game
Build the graph, the two prices, the signature, and the first-two-minutes scene before you build the board, the crew, or the vault. If a player who is alone in a server can close a spread, feel the hole regenerate, and leave an offer that the bot might fill, you have a game. The board, the booth, bulk, crews, and sourcing are how that game gets louder when people arrive. The vault is how the loudest version points at Robux without the minute-to-minute loop ever paying Robux.

If you add a fruit-style hunt before the signature exists, you will get players who wander, pick up objects, and leave, and RFY will record the bounce. If you add it after a player already hungers for a tag, the hunt is the most exciting version of a want they already have, and they will bring a friend because two people cover more nodes and split the haul on the floor afterward.

That is the whole design. One graph, one hole that refills, one number play can move, one number money can move, and a floor where other people’s wrong books are the content you never have to finish writing.
