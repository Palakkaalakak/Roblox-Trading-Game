# PART 1 — The Research: Normal Techniques, Dark Patterns, and Ultra-Dark Patterns

## 1.1 The behavioral backbone: operant conditioning and reward schedules

The foundational framework is Skinner's operant conditioning, applied to games through four reinforcement schedules. The practitioner's breakdown is well documented: **fixed ratio** (reward every N actions — reliable, transparent, used for mundane actions like XP per kill), **variable ratio** (reward after an unpredictable number of actions — the strongest habit-forming schedule, the engine behind mob drops and loot boxes), **fixed interval** (reward available once per time window — drives predictable return behavior, e.g., daily login bonuses and rested XP), and **variable interval** (reward available after unpredictable time — weak alone, powerful layered) [GameDeveloper](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/reward-schedules-and-when-to-use-them).

Two practitioner insights matter more than the taxonomy itself. First, **variable ratio works best when the reward is a bonus layered on top of gameplay, not a gate for continued gameplay** — players react positively when chance-rewards sit on a bedrock of activity they'd do anyway, and negatively when the chance *is* the activity [GameDeveloper](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/reward-schedules-and-when-to-use-them). This directly validates your second hard constraint: a variable-ratio mechanism as the *whole loop* is a slot machine; variable-ratio outcomes *modulating* a skill activity is a game. Second, **layering schedules** is where retention actually comes from — Warframe's daily login (fixed interval) with a randomized reward tier (variable ratio) is the canonical example [GameDeveloper](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/reward-schedules-and-when-to-use-them).

On the academic side, the variable-ratio schedule is the shared architecture of gambling and MMO reward structures — Karlsen's comparative analysis of gambling games and World of Warcraft found WoW's reward structure "comparable to gambling" in its psycho-structural elements, with the social layer (raids, guilds) doing additional entrapment work [Springer/Karlsen 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-010-9275-4). Neurobehavioral work confirms the mechanism: gambling systems are "based on variable-ratio schedules," and near-miss outcomes engage reward circuitry even though they are technically losses — the brain processes "almost won" as motivating rather than discouraging [Habib & Dixon, JEAB 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1901/jeab.2010.93-313). Systematic reviews confirm near-misses and losses-disguised-as-wins increase physiological arousal and continued play intent [Barton et al., Journal of Gambling Studies 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10899-017-9688-0), and experimental work shows near-miss effects exist in video games independent of gambling context [Finserås et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-019-00070-9). Your case-reel roll with a seeded high-value near-miss tile is a direct, shipped implementation of this literature — which is precisely why it is excluded from new proposals below.

## 1.2 The canonical dark pattern taxonomy

The foundational academic source is **Zagal, Björk & Lewis (FDG 2013), "Dark Patterns in the Design of Games"** (677 citations), which defines dark game design patterns as intentional design choices that trick players into spending something against their interests, organized into three categories [FDG 2013](http://www.fdg2013.org/program/papers/paper06_zagal_etal.pdf):

**Temporal dark patterns** — the player is deceived about time commitment:
- *Grinding*: repetitive, tedious tasks that substitute time-invested for skill, extending duration.
- *Playing by appointment*: the game dictates *when* you must play, often with "withering" — your assets decay if you don't return on schedule (FarmVille's crops).

**Monetary dark patterns** — the player is deceived about money:
- *Pay to skip*: monetizing the solution to artificially created friction.
- *Pre-delivered content*: content already on the disc gated behind an additional fee.
- *Monetized rivalries* (pay-to-win): selling competitive advantage, so spending becomes mandatory to maintain status.

**Social-capital dark patterns** — the player's relationships are the currency:
- *Social pyramid schemes*: progress requires recruiting others, converting friendship into a resource the game harvests.
- *Impersonation*: the game posts or messages as if it were the player, manufacturing social proof that never happened [FDG 2013](http://www.fdg2013.org/program/papers/paper06_zagal_etal.pdf).

Later work extends this. Aagaard et al. (CHI 2022, 92 citations) catalog dark patterns in mobile games across temporal, monetary, social, and psychological dimensions and — importantly — frame them as *engagement mechanics with a dial*, asking how the same levers can drive healthy engagement [ACM/CHI 2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3491101.3519837). Niknejad et al. (2024) map how these patterns shape mobile game structure end-to-end [ACM 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3701571.3701604). The 2024/2026 GaaS taxonomies of top-grossing games confirm these patterns are not fringe — they are the *core* monetization architecture of the highest-grossing free-to-play titles [deceptive.design](https://deceptive.design/articles/designing-for-monetization-a-taxonomy-of-dark-patterns-in-top-grossing-gaas-video-games/), and empirical player studies document real emotional and psychological impact, which is precisely the evidence that these levers *work* as designed [Veiga et al., ICEIS 2025](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2025/133658/133658.pdf).

## 1.3 The cognitive-bias layer (the "how" beneath the patterns)

Each named pattern above is a skin over a deeper, replicated cognitive bias. These are the transferable primitives:

**Loss aversion.** Kahneman & Tversky's prospect theory: losses loom roughly twice as large as equivalent gains. In games this manifests as streak preservation (login streaks convert engagement into obligation — breaking a streak *feels* like losing something you own), win-streak protection, lives systems, and "your rewards expire" framing [BehavioralEconomics.com](https://www.behavioraleconomics.com/resources/mini-encyclopedia-of-be/loss-aversion/) [Medium: Streaks and Habit Systems](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/streaks-and-daily-rewards-as-habit-forming-systems-dab7f5a34539). Puzzle games monetize failure itself by offering to sell back what a failed level "cost" you [LinkedIn: Loss Aversion in Puzzle Games](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/aslashcev_how-do-puzzle-games-use-loss-aversion-to-activity-7253710909724856320-S0I0).

**Endowment effect.** Ownership instantly inflates perceived value — people demand more to give up an object than they'd pay to acquire it. Games exploit this via starter items, trial gear, "your" island/plot, and asymmetric trade perception (what's *mine* is worth more than the identical item that's *yours*) [Psychology of Games](https://www.psychologyofgames.com/2010/02/the-endowment-effect-and-used-game-sales/) [Yu-kai Chou](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-study/endowment-effect-forces-ownership-possession/). Your asymmetric starter bundle is already a textbook endowment installation: the takeover screen makes ownership *dramatic*, and asymmetry means every player's endowed portfolio differs, which is what makes trade mutually attractive from minute one.

**IKEA effect.** Norton, Mochon & Ariely (2011, 2,125 citations): labor invested in creating something produces disproportionate valuation of the result. Players love builds, crafted items, and decorated spaces they *made*, independent of objective quality [Harvard Business School working paper](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/11-091.pdf). Documented in GaaS contexts specifically: effort-investment extends play duration because the player is defending the value of their own labor [Lohse, CHI PLAY 2020](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3436209.3436376). Your Crafter is an IKEA-effect machine: an item the player assembled from their own inputs is worth more *to them* than its market price, which creates deliberate bid-ask friction and market dynamism.

**Sunk cost fallacy.** Prior investment (time, money, effort) irrationally justifies continued investment. Games build this deliberately through incremental investment ladders; players "feel obligated to continue" to avoid writing off the accumulated investment [GameQuitters](https://gamequitters.com/how-the-sunk-cost-fallacy-keeps-you-playing-games/) [Design Bootcamp](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/product-design-and-psychology-the-exploitation-of-the-sunk-cost-fallacy-in-video-game-design-d60385e39fec). In your game, every item in the portfolio and every quest of the 9-quest chain is sunk investment anchoring the player to the ecosystem.

**Goal gradient + endowed progress.** Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006, 1,208 citations): people accelerate effort as they approach a goal. Nunes & Drèze (2006): *artificial* advancement toward a goal (a stamp card pre-loaded with 2 of 10 stamps) increases completion rates versus the identical 8-remaining task starting from zero [University of Chicago](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Goal-Gradient_Illusionary_Goal_Progress.pdf) [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1086/500480) [Learning Loop](https://learningloop.io/plays/psychology/endowed-progress-effect). Your ratcheted high-water-mark cashout bar is a goal-gradient engine with an elegant twist: the ratchet *removes the loss-aversion penalty* (a dip never moves the bar backward), so the player always perceives themselves at their historical best position — pure forward gradient.

**Zeigarnik effect.** Interrupted or incomplete tasks occupy memory more than completed ones — the "open loop" tension. Games leave deliberate incompleteness everywhere: 2-of-3 collectibles, a quest log with one unfinished entry, a chapter showing "2/3 quests" [Wikipedia: Zeigarnik effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect) [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/zeigarnik-effect) [r/ItsAllAboutGames](https://www.reddit.com/r/ItsAllAboutGames/comments/1r4b5k7/the_loop_of_incompleteness_how_the_zeigarnik/). Your 3-chapter × 3-quest chain with visible chapter progress is structurally Zeigarnik.

**Scarcity and urgency (FOMO).** Perceived scarcity inflates value; time-limited access triggers loss-aversion-driven impulsive action. Limited-time items, rotating stock, and expiring offers are the gaming implementations [Kadence](https://kadence.com/en-us/knowledge/the-psychology-of-fomo-and-how-brands-use-it-2/) [Instreamly: FOMO in gaming](https://instreamly.com/posts/fomo-marketing-how-brands-use-fear-of-missing-out-to-drive-conversions/). Player forums themselves now name this "artificial scarcity" — players *know* the mechanism, and it still moves behavior [ESO Forums](https://forums.elderscrollsonline.com/en/discussion/684653/fomo-needs-to-go) [r/The10thDentist](https://www.reddit.com/r/The10thDentist/comments/1uvjoyz/fomo_is_an_overused_term_and_limited_time_items/). Your rotating Gem Store and expiring Unique Crafts already run this.

**Anchoring and the decoy/compromise effect.** From Ariely's *Predictably Irrational*, applied directly to Roblox by developer RichOfPickles: price three products, place the one you want to sell in the middle, make the middle one visibly the best value; adding a "free" bonus to the middle product exploits the disproportionate psychological pull of zero-price [Roblox Developer Medium](https://medium.com/roblox-developer/from-the-devs-the-psychology-behind-player-retention-by-richofpickles-f46e13a691b1).

**Currency confusion / decoupling.** Premium currencies psychologically decouple spending from real-money value — players spend gems differently than they'd spend the equivalent Robux, and 100-gems-per-Robux arithmetic adds deliberate conversion friction. This is named explicitly in the loot-box literature as a persuasive strategy [Stehlík 2026, Charles University](https://dspace.cuni.cz/bitstream/handle/20.500.11956/211433/120541460.pdf?sequence=1).

**Social proof and status.** Leaderboards, floating profit titles, booth storefronts, and crew membership convert economic outcomes into publicly visible identity. Bartle's taxonomy explains *why* different players respond to different hooks: Achievers (accumulation, titles, ladders), Explorers (discovery, hidden knowledge), Socializers (crews, trade negotiation), Killers (dominance, out-trading others) — a trading economy game serves all four, which is rarer than it sounds [Wikipedia: Bartle taxonomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartle_taxonomy_of_player_types) [Extra Credits](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yxpW2ltDNow).

## 1.4 The "ultra-dark" tier: where pattern density becomes the product

The deepest layer is what recent literature calls monetization as *integrated design architecture* — not individual tricks but a system where the loop itself is engineered so that disengagement always feels like loss: the near-miss makes you roll again, the streak makes you log in, the expiring offer makes you buy now, the endowed portfolio makes quitting feel like abandoning property, and social obligation (crew goals, friends' visible progress) makes absence feel like letting people down [PubMed: Psychological mechanisms in video game monetization](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42113582/). The synthesis from loot-box platform research adds *monetized rivalries* and *currency confusion* as compounding layers on variable-ratio cores [Charles University 2026](https://dspace.cuni.cz/handle/20.500.11956/211433). Video analyses worth watching for the practitioner framing of all this: [Extra Credits — Dark Patterns](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SyeZf50jQXU), [Chris Wilson — Dark Patterns: Are Your Games Playing You?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OCkO8mNK3Gg), and [GDC 2017 — Dark Patterns: How Good UX Can Be Bad UX](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BYzwoqezAjU).

# PART 2 — The Distillation: Mechanisms That Map Onto ANY Game, and the Success Rubric

Stripped of genre, the research reduces to **ten portable mechanisms**. A mechanism is portable if it changes behavior through *structure* (pacing, framing, visibility) rather than through any specific content:

1. **Layered reward timing** — combine predictable return-triggers (fixed interval: dailies, rotations) with unpredictable outcome-magnitude (variable ratio) so that *when* to return is certain but *what you get* is not.
2. **Immediate feedback density** — the time between action and visible reward must be short at onboarding and can stretch with mastery, but never vanish.
3. **Visible, ratcheted progress** — every session must move some visible number, bar, or title forward, and ideally the display never punishes dips.
4. **Installed ownership early** — give the player things that are *theirs* within the first minute, asymmetrically if trading exists.
5. **Endowed head starts** — any long goal should begin with visible partial progress.
6. **Structured incompleteness** — always leave an open loop (an unfinished chapter, an unfilled collection slot, an expiring offer).
7. **Loss-framed return hooks** — something of value is forfeited or expires if the player doesn't return within a window.
8. **Effort-inflated valuation** — let players *make* things so they overvalue their own products (drives both retention and market spread).
9. **Public status conversion** — economic outcomes must become visible identity (titles, leaderboards, storefronts).
10. **Real-stakes amplification** — when progress converts to real value, every mechanism above intensifies; the cashout bar is the master goal that all sub-loops feed.

### Analytical Success Rubric (qualitative tiers; no fake precision)

Scoring tiers per row: **Absent → Functional → Strong → Signature.** The hypothesis — to be validated by playtesting and live metrics, not asserted as proven — is that a game scoring **Strong or better on every row** is structurally far more likely to retain and spread, because every documented retention lever has at least one competent implementation and nothing leaks motivation.

| # | Row | What "Strong" looks like | What "Signature" looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | One-sentence loop clarity | A stranger can state the core loop after watching 30 seconds | Players themselves explain it to each other unprompted |
| 2 | Time-to-first-reward | First meaningful win inside the first session's first minutes | First win is dramatic (a takeover, a reveal) and becomes a shareable story |
| 3 | Layered reward timing | At least one fixed-interval return hook + one variable-magnitude reward on top of activity | Multiple layered schedules across different timescales (minutes/hours/days) that players self-optimize around |
| 4 | Visible always-forward progress | A persistent progress display tied to the game's master goal | Progress display is loss-immune (ratcheted) and ambient (always on screen) |
| 5 | Ownership installation | Player owns asymmetric, personally meaningful assets in minute one | Ownership is *dramatized* (ceremony, takeover) so the endowment moment is memorable |
| 6 | Effort-created value | Players can make/transform items, not just receive them | Self-made items carry identity or provenance that others can see |
| 7 | Open loops | At least one visible unfinished objective at all times | Nested incompleteness (quest inside chapter inside title ladder) |
| 8 | Loss-framed returns | Something decays, expires, or rotates away on a daily-scale window | Multiple staggered expirations so almost any return time catches something |
| 9 | Social visibility of outcomes | Leaderboards or public markers of economic success | Status is ambient and unmissable (floating titles, storefronts) without opening a menu |
| 10 | Player-to-player value flow | Trading exists and is frictionless | Multiple trading channels (ads, order book, bulk fill, storefronts, 1:1) serving different Bartle types |

Against this rubric, your current design already scores Strong-or-better on most rows — asymmetric takeover starters (5), near-miss reel (3, partial), ratcheted cashout bar (4, Signature), four trading channels (10), rotating store and expiring crafts (8), floating profit titles (9). The weakest rows — where it likely sits at Functional — are **1, 2, 6, and 7**: after the tutorial's single guided craft, there is no *repeatable activity* the player performs with their hands; the loop clarity is "trade and roll," which is menu-driven rather than activity-driven. That gap is exactly what Part 3's proposal addresses.

# PART 3 — Roblox-Specific Adaptation and the Second Activity

## 3.1 The hard constraint, restated in my own words, and applied as a checklist

**Constraint, restated:** A gem can come into existence in exactly two ways: (1) a player buys a gem pack with real Robux, or (2) a player watches a rewarded ad, where the gems granted are strictly accounted at 10 gems per 1 Robux of ad revenue the developer receives. No other system may ever emit a gem. No NPC, quest, daily, AFK pad, leaderboard, crew perk, event, promo, or any other mechanic may add to a player's gem balance. Anything a system wants to "pay" a player must be paid in **items, materials, cosmetics, convenience (fee reductions, time reductions, extra capacity), or rank/title progress** — never gems. Below, every proposed mechanic carries an explicit payout check in the form *"pays in X, not gems."*

## 3.2 What Roblox specifically rewards (platform-level research)

## 3.4 The second activity: **Salvage Runs** (proposal)

Your game currently has a complete *economy* but no *verb*. Every interaction after the tutorial is a menu: craft in a panel, roll in a panel, list in a panel, trade in a panel. What's missing is the thing the player physically does between menu visits — the Blox-Fruits-shaped activity (solo, repeatable, effort-based, producing tradeable goods) without being a hunt, a boss, or combat.

**The proposal: Salvage Runs — instanced, timed scavenger expeditions into a shifting zone.**

**What the player physically does, moment to moment:** The player enters a procedurally re-arranged salvage zone (an instanced pocket map — a collapsed arcade, a flooded warehouse district, an abandoned mall — re-themed per season). Inside, **cache nodes** are scattered and partially hidden: behind geometry, up vertical climbs, inside simple environmental puzzles (a three-switch power sequence to open a shutter, a pressure-plate path, a keycode hinted by signage in the environment). Each cache is opened by a short **skill interaction**, not a button press: a timing-bar pry sequence where the success window shrinks on higher-value caches, a brief symbol-memory sequence flashed then recalled, or a steady-hand trace where the cursor must stay inside a moving channel. The player chooses their own route under a **run timer**: more distance and harder caches mean better hauls, but every player also carries a **satchel with limited slots** — forcing real decisions about which finds to keep, which to discard, and whether to push deeper or bank early at an **extraction gate**. Extracted contents convert to items/materials drawn **from the same reserve pools the economy already trades in** — so every Salvage Run is a faucet feeding the market, the Crafter, and the roll's item side. **Payout check: pays in items and materials from existing pools, not gems.**

**Where the skill lives:** movement and route-planning (spatial), pry/trace/memory interactions (timing and attention), satchel triage (choice under constraint), and the push-your-luck extraction decision (risk judgment). None of it resolves through a random number the player merely watches; the *player's execution* determines what a cache yields. Randomness exists only in *which* caches spawned where — the variable element modulates the activity rather than replacing it, exactly matching the literature's guidance that variable rewards should layer on top of gameplay, not gate it [GameDeveloper](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/reward-schedules-and-when-to-use-them).

**Why it feeds the economy instead of competing with it:** Salvage output skews toward crafting inputs and mid-tier trade goods — the items that make Bulk Buy requests fillable and give the Crafter recipe demand. High rollers still come from rolling; Salvage runs produce the *working capital* of the economy. This also gives Noobs a path to a saleable portfolio without spending, which powers the real-Robux aspiration loop: run → extract → list on Market → watch portfolio climb the ratcheted bar.

**The supporting systems (each with its constraint check):**

1. **Salvage tool tiers.** Players buy better pry tools / satchel expansions / zone-maps from an NPC. Better tools widen timing windows and add satchel slots — *time and error reduction, convenience*. **Pays in nothing** — this is a *sink*: tools are bought with items/materials (and premium tool skins via a gamepass). It emits no currency at all; it consumes items. Not gems.
2. **Run contracts.** A rotating board of effort contracts — "extract 12 copper-grade caches today," "finish a run with a full satchel" — paying in **material bundles and bonus cache spawns on the next run** (fee/time-equivalent value). These double as the daily/weekly quest *content* your post-chain quest system currently lacks. **Pays in materials and in-game advantages, not gems.** (Integrating contract progress into the existing daily/weekly quest framework grants rank-adjacent progress per your existing rules — quest rewards stay non-currency, as already designed.)
3. **Hard-cache seals.** Rare caches require a consumable seal. Seals come only from play (extracted rarely, or bought from other players on the Market) — creating a *consumable item sink* with player-priced value, the exact sink archetype Roblox economy veterans recommend [DevForum economy thread](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/how-to-design-the-ideal-in-game-economy/238862). **Pays in nothing; consumes items. Not gems.**
4. **Extraction tax waiver (subscription).** An existing-subscription perk: reduced item-conversion fee at the extraction gate — a fee reduction. **Pays in fee reduction, not gems.**
5. **Crew salvage goals** (Trader-tier). Crews pool extracted materials toward shared milestones unlocking cosmetic crew cache-skins. **Pays in cosmetics and crew perks, not gems.**
6. **AFK pads, resolved.** The undecided AFK reward tiers become **off-peak cache intel**: standing at a pad accrues map-hint tokens that reveal a few cache locations on your next run — a *time reduction* (less searching) rather than loot. AFK time converts into efficiency, not inventory, so the pads stop being a supply faucet the economy team has to balance. **Pays in time reduction (hints), not items and not gems.** This also makes AFK pads an on-ramp to Salvage Runs rather than a disconnected idle ladder.

**Psychology wiring (mechanisms informing pacing, per your second constraint):** the run timer + visible satchel-fill is a goal gradient inside every session (Kivetz et al. 2006 [UChicago](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Goal-Gradient_Illusionary_Goal_Progress.pdf)); the "one more cache before extraction" tension is structured incompleteness (Zeigarnik [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect)); the extraction decision loss-frames every run (Kahneman-Tversky [BehavioralEconomics.com](https://www.behavioraleconomics.com/resources/mini-encyclopedia-of-be/loss-aversion/)); items you *personally extracted and chose to keep* carry IKEA-effect and endowment premiums when you later list or trade them (Norton et al. 2011 [HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/11-091.pdf)); and the extraction ceremony (satchel dumps into a results tray) gives the same dramatic-feedback beat your takeover screen already proves works. Session-length-wise, a run supports both a 5-minute "quick shallow loop" and a 60-minute "deep route optimization" session — directly targeting rubric row R2.
